Compress a PDF to 100 KB

100 KB is a hard target. A one- or two-page text document gets there comfortably; a multi-page scan usually cannot without being flattened into images, which costs the selectable text. Type 100 KB, run it, and check the result before submitting — the tool reports the exact final size.

By Suprabhat · Updated September 21, 2026 · How we write these

Who asks for 100 KB?

  • Government and visa portals, which frequently cap a single document at 100 KB.
  • University and examination board application forms.
  • Older banking and insurance upload forms built around dial-up-era limits.
  • Job application systems that apply one cap to every attachment.

What to expect at 100 KB

A 1–2 page text document
Comfortable. Text and vector graphics are already tiny; there is usually nothing to lose.
A 1-page scan or photograph
Achievable. The image is recompressed and the resolution reduced; it stays readable on screen and in print.
A 5–10 page scan
Tight. Expect flattening, which means the text stops being selectable and searchable.
A 20+ page scan
Not realistic at readable quality. Split it and submit in parts, or ask whether the limit applies per file.

How to do it

  1. 1 Check whether the limit is per file or per form Many portals cap each attachment rather than the submission. If so, splitting a long document into two files is far better than crushing one into 100 KB.
  2. 2 Open Compress PDF and type 100 KB Pick "Online form → Under 100 KB" from the presets, or enter the figure yourself. The file is read by the page; nothing is uploaded.
  3. 3 Leave flattening off for the first attempt Without it, only the images are recompressed and the text layer survives. If the target is missed you will be told, and can decide knowingly.
  4. 4 Look at the before-and-after The result shows both versions of a page either side of a divider, with a measured score for what changed. Check the page with the smallest print — a footnote or a reference number.
  5. 5 Confirm the final size before you submit The exact byte count is shown. A portal that says 100 KB usually means 100,000 bytes, and a file at 102 KB will be rejected without explanation.

What flattening costs, and when to accept it

Below a certain size the only remaining saving is to stop storing a document and start storing a picture of one. Flattening does that: every page becomes a single image at a reduced resolution.

It always reaches the target, and it always costs the same three things — the text can no longer be selected, the document can no longer be searched, and a screen reader can no longer read it. For a form being checked by a person, that may be a fair trade. For anything that will be read back, archived or converted later, it is not.

Where the document has to stay searchable, the better move is to reduce what is in it: submit only the pages required, or split it, rather than flattening the lot.

Accessibility is the one nobody mentions: a flattened submission cannot be read aloud. If the recipient may need that, do not flatten.

Questions

Why is my PDF still over 100 KB after compressing?

Almost always because it is a multi-page scan. Each page is a photograph, and there is a floor below which a readable photograph cannot go. Either reduce the page count, or allow flattening and accept losing the text layer.

Does 100 KB mean 100,000 bytes or 102,400?

Portals almost always mean 100,000. Aim a little under — 95 KB rather than 99 — because a file that lands at 100.4 KB is rejected with the same unhelpful message as one at 5 MB.

Will the text still be selectable at 100 KB?

Yes, as long as flattening was not needed. Normal compression only recompresses images and leaves text and vector graphics untouched. If flattening was required to reach the target, the text becomes part of a picture.

Is a 100 KB PDF still good enough to print?

For a text document, yes — printing uses the text layer, which is unchanged. For a scan compressed this far, expect visible softening, which is usually acceptable for a form and not for anything that will be photocopied again.
