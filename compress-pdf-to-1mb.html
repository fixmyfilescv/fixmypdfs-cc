Compress a PDF to 1 MB

1 MB is a comfortable target for almost anything. A long scanned document reaches it with the text layer intact and no visible loss at normal viewing size, and a text document is usually there already. This is the default worth choosing when a limit is not imposed on you.

By Suprabhat · Updated September 21, 2026 · How we write these

Who asks for 1 MB?

  • Company email gateways, which are often stricter than the mail provider.
  • Client and customer portals accepting contracts or reports.
  • Web forms and helpdesk systems taking an attachment.
  • Anyone who simply wants a document that sends quickly on mobile data.

What to expect at 1 MB

A 50-page scan
Comfortable with the text layer kept and no visible loss on screen.
An illustrated report
Fits easily. Figures stay sharp at normal viewing size.
A photo brochure
Fits, with photographs slightly softened on close inspection.
A 300-page scan
Possible but aggressive. A larger target, or splitting, will read better.

How to do it

  1. 1 Type 1 MB as the target Or pick it from the presets. Nothing is uploaded — the file is read by the page you are on.
  2. 2 Run it, flattening off At 1 MB flattening is essentially never required, so the document keeps everything that makes it a document.
  3. 3 Check a figure or a photograph The before-and-after divider shows both versions of the page with a score for what changed. Illustrations show compression before text does.
  4. 4 Confirm the size and send The exact final size is reported. If the recipient has a stricter limit than you assumed, re-run from the original at a smaller target.

Why company email limits are lower than you expect

Gmail and most Microsoft 365 mailboxes allow 25 MB, but the gateway a business puts in front of its mail frequently allows far less — 10 MB is common and 5 MB is not unusual. The sender sees no warning until the message bounces, sometimes hours later.

There is a second trap: attachments are encoded for transit, which inflates them by roughly a third. A 24 MB file does not fit a 25 MB limit, and a 10.5 MB file does not fit a 10 MB one.

Targeting 1 MB sidesteps both. It clears every gateway anyone is likely to have, sends quickly on a phone, and leaves the document fully readable — which is why it is a better habit than compressing to whatever the limit happens to be.

Questions

Is 1 MB small enough for every email system?

For practical purposes, yes. Even strict corporate gateways allow several megabytes, and 1 MB leaves room for the transit overhead that catches people out near a limit.

Will anyone notice the difference at 1 MB?

On a text document, no — the text layer is untouched. On a photo-heavy document, only on close inspection of the images. Use the before-and-after view on your most detailed page if it matters.

My file is 1.1 MB. Is it worth compressing?

If a hard limit is forcing it, yes, and the cost will be negligible. If nothing is forcing it, leave it alone — the difference is not worth any loss at all.

Does compressing change the page count or order?

No. Compression only re-encodes what is inside the pages. The number of pages, their order, their size and the document structure are all unchanged.
